**Dated material, not legal advice.** The Venture Hacks posts are from 2010. Round sizes, instruments and norms
have changed since. Use them for the reasoning, and a startup lawyer for the terms.

## Raise after fit, not instead of it

His 2010 method says to iterate until you find product/market fit, not to raise money until you do, and then to
raise from high-quality people you trust while keeping control [VH-startup-principles].

## What investors weigh, in his order

> "I'd say in order of importance, it's probably traction then team then social proof then product."
> [VH-no-lead]

He gave the same order in a 2010 talk, adding market size after product [VH-presentation-hacks]. On traction:
"I think traction is the most important thing." [VH-presentation-hacks] On team: if you have traction and still
can't raise, he says, take a cold look in the mirror; maybe the team isn't that strong [VH-presentation-hacks].
On social proof: investors follow each other, like everyone else [VH-presentation-hacks].

## The pitch matters least

> "So here is my first piece of advice – the presentation doesn't matter." [VH-presentation-hacks]

What matters more, in that talk: the substance; a short, high-concept pitch; and knowing your customer at the
micro level, such as their churn and alternatives [VH-presentation-hacks]. When in doubt about the deck, "Go and
code. Build your product." [VH-presentation-hacks] In 2025 he says founders whose product shows well may do better
with a strong launch video and demo than with one-by-one investor meetings [NAV-curate-people].

## The founder raises

> "You can't outsource fundraising because investors are betting on you." [NAV-curate-people]

He adds that needing a banker to raise an early round is a symptom of a deeper problem [NAV-curate-people].

## Timing and state of mind

From 2026: he starts raising "preferably six to 12 months before we need it." [NAV-sell] He waits until his own
excitement about the business crosses a threshold, so he only has to convey what he already sees [NAV-sell], and
he doesn't dwell on investors who don't get it [NAV-sell].

## Terms and mechanics (2010)

- **Valuation.** Founders pricing their own round "invariably get it wrong, and usually it's too high."
  [VH-no-lead] Get data points from someone who sees many deals [VH-no-lead].
- **Party rounds.** For a round with no lead, he describes warm intros, name-dropping committed investors, and
  forcing functions [VH-no-lead]. A stated closing date is one forcing function; he warns that if you reach it with
  too little money, you lose credibility [VH-no-lead].
- **Preferred over notes, when you can.** He says it's probably better to do a preferred round, because early
  terms set precedent [VH-no-lead].
- **Ideas and NDAs.** The idea is no big deal, and asking an investor to sign an NDA filters out all but the most
  desperate [VH-a-conflict-of-interests].
- **Investor conflicts.** Define your space realistically narrowly; unless it's head-on competition, an investor
  in an adjacent company usually isn't a conflict [VH-a-conflict-of-interests].

## Who you take money from

From 2026: a bad investor is hard to walk away from, because they may sit on your board and hold preferred stock
[NAV-sell]. And the market has moved: "If your whole advantage is like, "Hey, I'm building cool software that other
people don't know how to build," I think that's uninvestable." [NAV-code] Our reading: investors now look for
hardware, network effects or AI models, which he lists [NAV-code], or for a team and traction that code alone
can't copy.

## How to apply it (our reading)

1. **Set the bar** before you raise: the traction number that would make you excited to pitch [NAV-sell].
2. **Start 6 to 12 months early** [NAV-sell]: build relationships and a short update list before you need money.
3. **Write the high-concept pitch** in one line, then know three customer numbers cold
   [VH-presentation-hacks].
4. **Get valuation data points** from someone who sees many rounds [VH-no-lead].
5. **Screen every investor** like a long-term partner (`templates/04-long-term-partner-check.md`) and every term
   sheet with `templates/09-deal-and-fundraise-check.md`.

**Worked number (invented).** A pre-seed SaaS team has $18,000 of monthly recurring revenue growing 12% a month,
and burns $45,000 a month with $400,000 in the bank: about nine months of runway. Starting to raise now is inside
his six-to-twelve-month window; waiting three months would leave six. Raising $1.5M at a $12M post-money sells
12.5%. A "hot" offer of $2M at $20M post with a board seat and a 2x participating preference sells only 10% but
constrains every future round. Our reading: the board seat and preference are the long-term terms; the
valuation gap is the small split. See `examples/02-pre-seed-founder.md`.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- Polishing the deck instead of the product [VH-presentation-hacks].
- Raising at the last minute, when you can't walk away.
- Optimizing headline valuation and accepting terms that trap you [NAV-sell].
- Pitching pure software as the moat in 2026 [NAV-code].

**Limits:** this is US-style venture fundraising. Most businesses shouldn't raise venture money at all; a
profitable business you own outright fits his wealth definition (chapter 01) just as well.

**Use it now:** `templates/09-deal-and-fundraise-check.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. Do you have the traction that would make you excited to pitch? If not, what's the number?
2. How many months of runway do you have? Is that inside the 6 to 12 month window?
3. Which term in your current offer would be hardest to undo in three years?
4. Could you explain your customer's churn and alternatives without notes?
