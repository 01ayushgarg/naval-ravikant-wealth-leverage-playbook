## What it is

> "Arm yourself with specific knowledge, accountability, and leverage."
> [TWEET18 #13](https://x.com/naval/status/1002104865919664128)

The next tweet defines it as knowledge you can't be trained for, because whatever society can train you for, it
can train someone else for, and replace you [TWEET18 #14](https://x.com/naval/status/1002104947624722433). His
2008 wording was knowing how to do something society can't yet easily train others to do [NAV-life-formulas-i].
In 2025 he quoted tweet #14 and extended it to machines: "Specific knowledge doesn't have verifiably correct
answers - so it's not something AI can be trained for, either." [X 2025-12-24](https://x.com/naval/status/2003747849264300469)

> "And it's not true that everything can be taught. In fact, the most interesting things cannot be taught."
> [RICH-13]

Why "specific" and not "unique": "it can only be built as part of a larger obsession, interest, and time spent
in that domain." [RICH-14]

## You mostly find it, you don't choose it

> "You almost have to look back on your own life and see what you're actually good at." [RICH-13]

> "So, very often, your specific knowledge is observed and often observed by other people who know you well and
> revealed in situations rather than something that you come up with." [RICH-13]

His own case: his mother, watching him critique how a local pizza parlour sold its slices, told him as a teenager
that he'd go into business [RICH-13]. In 2025 he adds that you find it by doing: a friend who'll call hundreds of
people to get one yes has found his [NAV-find]. And working for yourself pulls you toward it, because you
naturally pick work that fits who you are [NAV-find]. A 2019 post summing up the podcast says to build it where
you are "a natural" [X 2019-03-30](https://x.com/naval/status/1112012346933665792).

## Go all in, or lose to someone who does

> "So, if you're not 100% into it somebody else who is 100% into it will outperform you." [RICH-13]

In 2025 he puts the bar higher still: to be the top, you have to enjoy the thing to an almost absurd degree
[NAV-enjoy]. Once you have it, he says, credentials matter less: "You are ready to seize any role in life once
you have the specific knowledge and overwhelming desire - experience is irrelevant." [X 2022-02-12](https://x.com/naval/status/1492594442074836995)

## Don't assemble it too deliberately, but do combine

> "But I think if you go around trying to build it a little too deliberately, if you become too goal-oriented
> on the money, then you won't pick the right thing." [RICH-14]

On combining skills (the skill-stack idea, which Nivi credits to Scott Adams [RICH-14]): "So, it's much easier
to be top 5 percentile at three or four things than it is to be literally the number one at something."
[RICH-14]

## Apprenticeship, and the steepest learning curve

He says that to the extent it's taught, it's on the job, through apprenticeships [RICH-14]. If you can't get one:
"Find the part of the job with the steepest learning curve." [NAV-finding-time] And: "You build specific
knowledge by taking accountability for things that other people don't know how to do." [NAV-finding-time]

## Timely and timeless

> "Timeless specific knowledge usually can't be taught, and it sticks with you forever. Timely specific
> knowledge comes and goes; but it tends to have a fairly long shelf life." [NAV-finding-time]

His examples: machine learning learned out of real interest just as it takes off is timely; persuasion is
timeless [NAV-finding-time]. A tell he names: someone who can't give a straight answer about what they do every
day may be doing something nobody can be trained for [NAV-finding-time].

## A niche big enough

> "So, whatever niche obsession you have, the internet allows you to scale." [RICH-10]

He suggests an audience of about 50,000 passionate people can be enough [RICH-10].

## How to apply it (our reading)

1. **Collect evidence, not wishes.** Write down what people come to you for, unprompted, over the last two
   years. Ask three people who know you well [RICH-13].
2. **Strike out anything a six-month course could teach.** What's left is a candidate.
3. **Tag each candidate** as timely or timeless [NAV-finding-time], and technical or creative
   ([#18](https://x.com/naval/status/1002105243767787520)).
4. **Combine two or three** into one stack where you're near the top few percent [RICH-14].
5. **Test it by doing** something public and small with it within 30 days [NAV-find]. If nobody responds, go
   back to step 1.

**Worked scenario (invented).** Marco is a hospital pharmacist who also writes small scripts to clean up the
ward's drug-interaction spreadsheets, and nurses keep asking him to explain dosing changes. Candidates: pharmacy
(trainable, licensed), spreadsheet scripting (trainable), explaining dosing to busy nurses (not easily trained).
His stack is pharmacology plus light code plus plain explanation. Test: a one-page weekly dosing note for one
ward, and a small checker tool. After 30 days, two other wards ask for it. That pull is the evidence.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- Picking a hot field because it pays now, the thing #15 warns against.
- Mistaking a credential for specific knowledge. If thousands share the credential, it's trainable.
- Never testing it, so it stays a self-description.
- Stacking skills nobody pays for together.

**Limits:** he notes that most people over about 20 don't get to choose their specific knowledge [RICH-13]. Our
reading: that's a reason to look back carefully, not to give up.

**Checks to run:**
1. What do you do that feels like play to you and looks like work to others? Ask three people who know you well.
2. Could a course teach someone your job in six months? If yes, what part of it couldn't they learn that way?
3. Which of your skills are timely, which are timeless, and which pair could you combine?
4. Is there an audience of 50,000 people who care about your niche? Where do they gather?
