## The most important tweet, in his view

> "You're not going to get rich renting out your time."
> [TWEET18 #5](https://x.com/naval/status/1002103670400417792)

The rest of the tweet says you must own equity, a piece of a business, to gain financial freedom
[TWEET18 #5](https://x.com/naval/status/1002103670400417792). In 2020, asked which tweet matters most, he called it
"the most important foundational tweet." [TF-473]

## Why renting out your time doesn't get you there

> "So, what that means is when you're sleeping, you're not earning. When you're retired, you're not earning."
> [RICH-06]

His contrast: one great engineer's hour can be worth a fortune, while the best lumberjack is only a few times
better than the worst [RICH-06]. What you want instead is work "where your inputs don't match your outputs."
[RICH-06]

## Equity is the risk position, so it gets the upside

He walks down the capital stack: employees are paid first, then lenders, then owners [RICH-20].

> "The equity holders take on greater risk, but in exchange, they get nearly unlimited upside." [RICH-20]

Whoever sets your pay is asking how replaceable you are; with high accountability, he says, they have to offer
you a piece of the upside [RICH-20].

## Routes to owning a piece

Stock options at a tech company are "a fine way to start." [RICH-06] In 2020 he added that without owning a piece
of a business, it's going to be extremely hard to get wealthy [TF-473]. The routes he describes [ANGEL-1]:

| Route | What it takes | His caution, paraphrased |
|---|---|---|
| Start a company | Most equity, most risk | Being a founder for life is a tough road most people aren't built for [ANGEL-1] |
| Join as a proven operator | A reputation for execution | Be known as the person who gets the call when the next winner scales [ANGEL-1] |
| Invest | Capital, judgement, dealflow (chapter 12) | As outcomes get more nonlinear, investing makes more sense relative to founding [ANGEL-1] |

## Think like an owner before you are one

> "Train yourself how to think like a principal, and eventually you will become a principal."
> [NAV-principal-agent]

From the other side, he says the hardest thing for a founder is finding employees with a founder mentality, which
he glosses as caring enough [NAV-finding-time]. If you're the employee, the skills you gain that way are part of
your pay [NAV-finding-time].

## If you're the owner: share it

He says to be generous with top lieutenants [NAV-principal-agent]. His 2011 line on pre-traction startups:
"Your first two engineers? They're just late founders. Treat them as such." [NAV-why-you-cant-hire] (The
percentages in that post are 2011 Silicon Valley numbers.) In 2025 he says founders can break rules around the cap
table to get the best people [NAV-curate-people]. For co-founders he recommends founder vesting, which he calls the
pre-nup [VH-pick-cofounder].

## The mechanics, in plain terms (our reading, not his words)

| Term | What it usually means | What to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Shares vs options | Shares you own now; options are a right to buy shares later at a set price (the strike) | Which am I getting? |
| Percentage | Your shares ÷ fully diluted shares (including the option pool) | Fully diluted count? |
| Vesting | You earn the equity over time, often four years | Schedule and cliff? |
| Cliff | Nothing vests until a first date, often one year | What if I leave or I'm let go before it? |
| Exercise window | Time to buy vested options after you leave; can be short | How long, and what will it cost? |
| Dilution | Each new round issues shares and shrinks your percentage | Expected rounds before an exit? |
| Preferences | Investors may get their money back before common holders | Total preference stack? |

**Worked number (invented).** Lena is offered 0.40% of a seed-stage company, as options, four-year vesting,
one-year cliff, in place of $20,000 a year of salary. Say two more rounds each dilute her by 20%: 0.40% × 0.8 ×
0.8 = 0.256%. A $500M exit pays her about $1.28M before tax and strike costs, if preferences are covered. A $50M
exit pays about $128,000, less than the $80,000 of salary she gave up plus the risk. Most startups return
nothing. Our reading: take startup equity for the learning and the chance of the big outcome, not as a safe
swap for salary, and only on terms you understand. That fits his power-law view (chapter 12).

**How to apply it (our reading):**
1. List every piece of equity you own today, with your best estimate of its value.
2. For any offer, fill in the mechanics table above before you negotiate.
3. Ask for the fully diluted count and the preference stack. If they won't say, that's information.
4. Model three outcomes (zero, a modest sale, a big win) and check you can live with zero.
5. If you employ people, check that those who act like principals own like principals [NAV-principal-agent].

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- Treating a percentage as money before dilution and preferences.
- Leaving before the cliff, or being unable to afford to exercise. He mentions this exact objection when
  recruiting [NAV-curate-people].
- Taking advisory shares that cost more time than they're worth (chapter 15).
- Owning nothing for a decade because equity felt risky.

**Limits:** most equity ends at zero, and his own figures come from Silicon Valley. See chapter 18.

**Checks to run:**
1. If you stopped working for a year, what would still pay you? That's your equity.
2. Do your outputs scale with your hours, or can one good hour be worth a thousand?
3. Which route to ownership fits you: founder, early operator, or investor?
4. For any equity you hold: what's it worth after two more rounds, and what happens if you leave tomorrow?
