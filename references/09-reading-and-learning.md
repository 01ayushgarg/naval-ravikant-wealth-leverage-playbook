## The three tweets, our paraphrase

- There's no skill called "business"; skip business magazines and classes
  ([#31](https://x.com/naval/status/1002107808202960896)).
- Study microeconomics, game theory, psychology, persuasion, ethics, mathematics and computers
  ([#32](https://x.com/naval/status/1002107869209096192)).
- Reading is faster than listening, and doing is faster than watching
  ([#33](https://x.com/naval/status/1002107992018333696)).

## Reading is the foundation

> "the foundation of learning is reading. I don't know a smart person who doesn't read and read all the time."
> [RICH-16]

The tweet he says he left out of the thread: "read what you love until you love to read." [RICH-16] He posted
it in April 2019 with a coda: "The means of learning are abundant, it's the desire to learn that's scarce."
[X 2019-04-04](https://x.com/naval/status/1113621557866303488) How much he
reads, in 2017: "I probably read one to two hours a day." [TKP-18] And: "Making it an actual habit is the most
important thing." [TKP-18]

## Read foundations, slowly, and reread

He says to read foundational works (Adam Smith rather than a business book, Darwin rather than a new biology
book) [RICH-16], and that mathematics and logic are the ultimate foundation [RICH-17].

> "It's better to go through a book really slowly and struggle and stumble and rewind, than it is to fly through
> it quickly" [RICH-17]

On rereading, he quotes @illacertus wanting to read the best 100 books over and over [NAV-read]. In 2025 he
splits it: Lindy books for human nature, the bleeding edge for specific knowledge you get paid for
[NAV-density], and he narrows the foundation to
"just say study physics." [NAV-simplest] His 2026 filter: "If it's not one of the best books you've ever read,
don't read it." [X 2026-05-20](https://x.com/naval/status/2057059290553155741)

## The five skills

He names reading, writing, arithmetic and persuasion, then adds programming as the fifth [RICH-17]:

> "If you're good with computers, if you're good at basic mathematics, if you're good at writing, if you're good
> at speaking, and if you like reading, you're set for life." [RICH-17]

## Understand, don't memorize

> "You want to be able to describe it in 10 different ways, in simple sentences from the ground up and re-derive,
> whatever you need. If you just memorize, you're lost." [TF-473]

The same idea applied to reading lists: "Instead of asking what books you should read, ask what ideas you should
understand." [X 2024-08-31](https://x.com/naval/status/1829908521489744117)

## Doing is the faster teacher

> "It's the number of iterations that drives the learning curve." [RICH-18]

He compressed it in 2022: "It's not 10,000 hours, it's 10,000 iterations." [X 2022-11-22](https://x.com/naval/status/1594923336043069441)

He adds that you have to get comfortable with frequent small failures [RICH-18]. In 2025: "Life is lived in the
arena. You only learn by doing." [NAV-arena] And: "So I think doing leads to the desire to learn and therefore to
learning." [NAV-do]

The two mental models he says matter most: the principal-agent problem, and the tit-for-tat iterated prisoner's
dilemma [RICH-18].

## Treat advice as hooks, not instructions

He says the best founders he knows listen to everyone, then ignore everyone and make up their own minds
[RICH-34]. He also says most of his tweets are maxims meant as mental hooks for when a situation recurs [RICH-34].

## The one-hour-a-day line

> "Reading science, math, and philosophy one hour per day will likely put you at the upper echelon of human
> success within seven years." [X 2017-06-04](https://x.com/naval/status/871415571551629312)

This 2017 post is the primary source for the Almanack's compiled version of the line [ALM, compiled by Eric
Jorgenson]. In an interview the same year he says he reads one to two hours a day and that most people don't read
an hour a day [TKP-18].

## How to apply it (our reading)

1. **Set a daily reading floor** (30 to 60 minutes) and make it a habit before you choose the books [TKP-18].
2. **Pick one foundation book** in your field and read it slowly, rewinding when lost [RICH-17].
3. **Pick one doing project** that gives you at least five iterations a week [RICH-18].
4. **Explain one idea five ways** each week, from first principles [TF-473].
5. **Run every piece of advice** through his filter in chapter 10 before acting on it [RICH-34].

**Worked scenario (invented).** A junior data analyst spends 6 hours a week on business podcasts. Swap: 5 hours
of reading (one statistics textbook, slowly; one classic on persuasion), plus a weekly mini-analysis published
internally. After three months: 12 published analyses (12 iterations with feedback) instead of 78 hours of
listening.

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- Reading for the count, not understanding.
- Collecting advice and never testing it.
- Courses as a substitute for doing.
- Skipping the foundations because they feel slow.

**Use it now:** the "foundations" section of `templates/01-specific-knowledge-finder.md`.

**Checks to run:**
1. Which foundational book in your field have you never read in the original?
2. What are you learning by doing right now, and how many iterations a week does it give you?
3. Could you explain your core skill five ways from first principles, or have you memorized it?
4. Which piece of advice are you following that you've never tested against your own context?
