## Reading is the foundation

> "the foundation of learning is reading. I don't know a smart person who doesn't read and read all the time."
> [RICH-16]

The tweet he says he left out of the thread: "read what you love until you love to read." [RICH-16]

> "It's not the means of education or the means of learning are scarce, the means of learning are abundant. It's
> the desire to learn that's scarce." [RICH-16]

## Read foundations, slowly

> "So, it is important that you read foundational things." [RICH-16] His examples: Adam Smith instead of a
> business book, Darwin instead of a new biology book, Feynman for physics [RICH-16].

> "The ultimate foundation are mathematics and logic." [RICH-17]

> "It's better to go through a book really slowly and struggle and stumble and rewind, than it is to fly through
> it quickly" [RICH-17]

> "If you understand the basics, especially in mathematics and physics and sciences, then you will not be afraid
> of any book." [RICH-16]

On rereading, he quotes @illacertus: "I don't want to read all the books; I just want to read the best 100 over
and over again." [NAV-read]

On what to read in 2025: "When it comes to anything about human nature, I say go read the Lindy books" [NAV-density]
"But if you want to develop specific knowledge, get paid for it, do something useful, then you want to stay on
the bleeding edge." [NAV-density] And: "But the best authors respect the reader's time" [NAV-density].

In 2025 he narrows the foundation further: "I would summarize that further and just say study physics."
[NAV-simplest] Because "physics trains you to interact with reality, and it is so unforgiving that it beats all
the nice falsities out of you." [NAV-simplest]

## The five skills

> "I would say that the five most important skills are of course, reading, writing, arithmetic, and then as
> you're adding in, persuasion, which is talking." [RICH-17] He adds programming as the fifth [RICH-17].

> "If you're good with computers, if you're good at basic mathematics, if you're good at writing, if you're good
> at speaking, and if you like reading, you're set for life." [RICH-17]

From 2016: "Your most important skill isn't even what you majored in or even what you studied, it's just
knowing how to learn." [TF-QA]

## Understand, don't memorize

> "You want to be able to describe it in 10 different ways, in simple sentences from the ground up and re-derive,
> whatever you need. If you just memorize, you're lost." [TF-473]

> "Running a lemonade stand will teach you better accounting than a textbook." [TF-473]

## Doing is the faster teacher

> "So, the learning curve is across iterations" [RICH-18], not repetitions.

> "It's the number of iterations that drives the learning curve." [RICH-18]

> "So you just have to get very, very comfortable with frequent small failures." [RICH-18]

> "Life is lived in the arena. You only learn by doing." [NAV-arena]

> "So the right way to learn is to actually go do something, and when you're doing it, you figure something out
> about how it should be done." [NAV-arena]

Nivi reads out the tweet behind that episode: "Acquiring knowledge is easy, the hard part is knowing what to
apply and when." [NAV-arena, Nivi reading his tweet]

From his newest company: "So I think doing leads to the desire to learn and therefore to learning." [NAV-do]

## The two mental models he says matter most

> "my personal learning has been that the principal-agent problem drives so much in this world." [RICH-18]

> "tit-for-tat iterated prisoner's dilemma is the piece of game theory that is worth knowing the most." [RICH-18]

## Treat advice as hooks, not instructions

> "The best founders I know read and listen to everyone. But then they ignore everyone and make up their own
> mind." [RICH-34]

> "If you survey enough people, all of the advice will cancel to zero." [RICH-34]

> "Ninety percent of my tweets are maxims that become mental hooks to remind me when I'm in that situation again."
> [RICH-34]

within seven years." [ALM, compiled by Eric Jorgenson] We couldn't trace it to a primary source in this corpus, so
treat it as the compiler's selection.

**Checks to run:**
1. Which foundational book in your field have you never read in the original?
2. What are you learning by doing right now, and how many iterations a week does it give you?
3. Could you explain your core skill five ways from first principles, or have you memorized it?
4. Which piece of advice are you following that you've never tested against your own context?
