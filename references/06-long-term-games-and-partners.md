## Compound interest, everywhere

The thread says to pick an industry where you can play long-term games with long-term people
([#7](https://x.com/naval/status/1002103770518441989)), and that all the returns in life, in wealth,
relationships or knowledge, come from compound interest in iterated games
([#9](https://x.com/naval/status/1002103908947263488)).

> "You have to be able to play a long-term game. And long-term games are good not just for compound interest,
> they're also good for trust." [RICH-09]

Nivi's summary, which Naval called "a brilliant formulation" [RICH-09]: "In a long-term game, it seems that
everybody is making each other rich. And in a short-term game, it seems like everybody is making themselves
rich." [RICH-09, Nivi]

What compounding buys: less friction, so you can do bigger things together [RICH-09]. With someone he has worked
with for 20 years, he says, they don't have to read the legal contracts [NAV-relationships]. And the cost of
leaving your field: "every time you wander out of where you built your network, you're going to be starting from
scratch." [RICH-09]

## Pick partners on three things

> "Pick business partners with high intelligence, energy, and, above all, integrity."
> [TWEET18 #10](https://x.com/naval/status/1002103969781448704)

He credits the three-part test to Warren Buffett [ANGEL-2]. His reasons, paraphrased [RICH-11]: without
intelligence they head the wrong way; without energy you get smart, lazy people; without integrity you have a
smart, hard-working crook who will eventually cheat you.

### Read signals, not claims

> "And signals is what they do, not what they say." [RICH-11]

His tells: how someone treats a waiter is how they'll eventually treat you [RICH-11]; the more someone says they're
moral, the less likely they are to be [RICH-11]. From 2016: "If you see someone who's always fighting with
somebody else, they're eventually going to fight with you." [TF-QA]

And energy for this thing in particular: he won't start a company or hire someone who isn't into what he wants
them to do [RICH-11].

### No cynics

The thread warns against partnering with cynics and pessimists, because their beliefs are self-fulfilling
([#11](https://x.com/naval/status/1002104083694501890)). On the podcast: "Essentially, to create things, you have
to be a rational optimist." [RICH-12]

## The one-day rule

What he called in 2016 his most popular tweet: "If you can't see yourself working with somebody for life, don't
work with them for a day." [TF-QA] In practice, once he knows he won't be working with someone in ten years, he
starts investing less in the relationship [RICH-34].

## Ethics as long-term greed

> "If you cut people fair deals, you won't get paid in the short term. But over the long term, everybody will
> want to deal with you." [NAV-ethics]

The fastest way out of the game, in his view, isn't betting too much but "cutting corners and doing unethical or
downright illegal things." [NAV-kelly-criterion]

## When you're stuck in a one-off game

> "If someone is taking advantage of you in a negotiation, your best option is to turn it from a short-term game
> into a long-term game." [NAV-short]

His moves, with a house renovation as the example [NAV-short]: split the work into two projects and decide on the
second after the first; mention friends waiting to see how it goes; leave a public review. And: "Negotiations are
won by whoever cares less." [NAV-short]

## How to apply it (our reading)

1. **List your long-term people:** those you'd work with for 20 years. Count hours a week spent with them.
2. **Score each key partner** on intelligence, energy and integrity, using things you've seen them do
   [RICH-11]. A low integrity score fails the whole test.
3. **Run the one-day rule** on each relationship that takes real time [TF-QA].
4. **Turn one-off deals into repeated ones**: smaller first projects, shared networks, public reputations
   [NAV-short].
5. **Stay in your field** long enough for trust to compound [RICH-09].

**Worked scenario (invented).** A two-person design studio has three clients. Client A has paid on time for six
years and refers work. Client B pays 30% more but disputes every invoice. Client C is new. Our reading of his
rules: protect A, give C a small first project to start a repeated game, and either reset B's terms or let B go,
since a partner who fights everyone will eventually fight you [TF-QA].

**Failure modes (our reading):**
- Judging integrity by charm or by what people say about themselves [RICH-11].
- Staying in a relationship you already know won't last ten years.
- Switching industries every two years and losing the compound.
- Treating fair dealing as weakness in a short game you could have made long.

**Limits:** long games need a stable place to play. In fast-churning markets or short contracts you may have to
build the repeated game yourself, which is what NAV-short is about.

**Checks to run:**
1. Name the people you'd want to work with for the next 20 years. How much of your week goes to them?
2. For your closest partner or co-founder: what have they done, under pressure, that shows integrity?
3. Who in your circle is a cynic? What is it costing your plans?
4. Which current deal is a one-off game, and how could you turn it into a repeated one?
